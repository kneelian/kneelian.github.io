MS-DOS and You, pt. 1

enn

2025-09-30 16:16

Historium

There's probably no need to introduce MS-DOS itself, but it's more fun to do that than to treat it seriously, because it wasn't a serious (in the comedy sense, not respect) operating system. It's also one ingredient in the success story of the x86 instruction set.

Ignoring its weird origins as a derivative of an OS that Microsoft bought, then relicenced to everyone and also intentionally crippled to not compete with its Unix offering, MS-DOS became the success it was and the foundation of Microsoft's business owing to their long-term collaboration with IBM on what became the baseline of modern home computation (the IBM PC). Throughout its history it morphed from the main OS you'd run to just a bootloader for Windows, and then the ecosystem gently transitioned out of it into the Windows of today. This gradual, incremental rate of change also ensures that Windows of today still carries some parts of its legacy, such as reserved file and folder names referring to old devices.

MS-DOS is generally synonymous with DOS, even though other DOSes (both by Microsoft and by other vendors) existed, and many of which weren't even compatible with MS-DOS or ran on other types of devices. MS-DOS has become the default DOS, and many [1] modern DOS projects like FreeDOS aim for compatibility with it, not with other systems of the time like TurboDOS. This is stupid, there are excellent systems at the time that were both compatible with MS-DOS and offered great upgrades over it (like, for example, multitasking), but it is how it is. For the rest of this post I'll just write DOS for what really is MS-DOS.

The thing about DOS is that it's a 16-bit operating system running on chips capable of more than that usually; with the release of the 80386 in 1985, it became resident on a 32-bit capable chip. The first few DOSes ran on chips without memory management and protection (8086 and 80186), which let you just go wild on your entire system memory if you weore so inclined. The introduction of the 80286 brought protected mode to the ecosystem, but DOS itself remained a 16-bit OS running in real mode; to allow programs access to things like more than 1M of RAM, protected mode must be employed, and DOS had no native way of doing this. This is where DPMI [2] comes in, and is a core component of basically all DOS memory extenders (and of all the ones I'm familiar with). Without it, you're stuck with approximately 640K of what's called conventional memory (because by convention all IBM PC-compatible devices let you have that much ), which is a nightmare for more sophisticated software.

DOS really kind of overstayed its welcome. It was around for so damn long that software written for it was perplexingly extensive, and whole workforces were so trained on it that retraining or replacing them was prohibitively expensive. Unlike Vista, it left a gargantuan long-term impact on nearly every part of business and software, and some business spheres still use it to this day [3] without realistically having a replacement plan.

Why DOS (aka, target)

We found some old computers collecting dust over in the university refuse pile, and decided to dust them off and bring them home. They're all over 20 years old, and are running DOS, or can be massaged into running it with little worry. I figured, why not actually put that rusty hardware to good use and make it move images around and flash me some lights in unison?

Like the venerable mr. Chen says, "MS-DOS could do graphics, in the sense that it didn’t actively prevent you from doing graphics", which is actually more than enough for many people. A hundred thousand pieces of DOS software can attest to this, and people devised so many brilliant (or, rather, horrifying) solutions to the problems artificially generated by having to work with such a Frankensteinian operating system and hardware combination.

The device I'm targetting is a NASA supercomputer compared to what people during the heyday of DOS had available to them. It's a Pentium 2 with 128M of RAM and a bunch of other modern bells and whistles.
